Does a predicted structure dock well enough to match a crystal?

Structure prediction can now give you a protein model for almost any sequence. If you want to know how a drug binds to it, you still have to dock the drug into that model, and the honest question is whether the answer resembles what an X-ray experiment would have shown you.

This page is one campaign that asks exactly that. The target is EGFR, the drug is erlotinib, and the answer is already known: crystal structure 1M17 shows where erlotinib really sits. So every prediction here can be marked against it.

The measure throughout is RMSD, the average distance between the predicted drug and the real one. Under 2 Å is normally called a success; 8 Å is the width of the pocket, and means the prediction is simply in the wrong place.

Six runs, two things changed

Everything was held constant except two choices: where the protein model came from, and which docking method searched it. Each run links to its full scorecard.

What it means

Docking straight into an AlphaFold model did not work

All three AlphaFold runs put erlotinib between 4.9 and 8.4 Å from where it really binds. Changing the docking method did not rescue them. The reason is not that the protein model is bad — its binding site is within 1 Å of the crystal — but that the docking search never generated a good answer to choose from. Nothing later in the pipeline can recover a right answer that was never proposed.

Folding the protein together with the drug did work

The winning runs do something different first: they fold the protein and the drug together, then throw the predicted drug position away and keep only the protein. That protein has a pocket shaped around this drug, and docking into it finds the right answer — 1.59 and 1.66 Å, both graded B.

The predicted drug position is discarded on purpose: it is 4.5 to 4.8 Å out on its own. The prediction is good at building the pocket and poor at placing the molecule inside it, so this pipeline takes the pocket and re-does the placement.

Picking the right answer matters as much as finding it

Docking returns ten candidate positions and ranks them. That ranking is often wrong: in one run the correct position was there but ranked eighth, and the run scored a D. A second scoring function now re-ranks the ten and chooses which goes forward, which turned that same run into a B without changing anything else.

The slowest option was the worst option

"Flexible" docking, which lets the protein move during the search, took seven times longer than standard docking and produced the poorest result on both protein models. More computation is not more accuracy.

The short version

  • Fold the protein with the drug, then re-dock 1.6 Å from the crystal
  • Dock into an AlphaFold model directly 4.9 to 8.4 Å
  • Let a second scorer choose the pose turned a D into a B
  • Spend seven times longer on flexible docking worst result in both arms

One target and one drug, six runs. This is a worked example, not a benchmark: it shows what the pipeline measures and how the answers differ, and the same campaign on another target could come out differently. That is the point of checking every prediction against a structure rather than trusting a docking score.
